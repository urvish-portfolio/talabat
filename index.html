2Accepted

A team that knows every product

Accepting an order is a promise that the team can make it right. At Shake Shack and Fresh Bake, food knowledge was trained in three steps, and nobody moved on until every item was right.

  • Learn: the brand's online modules on recipes, procedures and quality checks.
  • Make and rate: before opening the next day, each person made the items and rated them against the standard.
  • Prove it on the floor: random questions during service, with a recognition card for every right answer and every good catch.

The rule the team worked by: asking the manager on duty is always welcome; sending a guest the wrong product is not.

3Preparing

Seeing problems before the screen does

The company's travel path is a 13-point walk from the front door through the hot side, the pass, prep, cold storage and the staff area. I walked it every 90 minutes to two hours, so a short station, a warm chiller or a missing label showed up before an order did.

Prep followed the forecast for the day. Each morning's numbers set the prep levels, the rota and who stood where at peak.

What the walk checks

Stations stockedbefore the rush, never during it
Temperatureshot holding and chillers, logged
Labels and datesso nothing expired reaches a bag
The passorders checked before they leave

An area manager's line I still use: if the manager has to go on station, he is not managing well.

4Ready at the pass

International Burger Day, 28 May 2025

Buy one, get one free on every burger. The queue was forming behind the shutter by 10:30. At 11:00, as we opened, a delivery of about £4,000 arrived at the back door, and fresh and frozen stock could not wait outside.

I sent the two team members from the pass to put the delivery away and ran the pass alone, with 15 to 20 orders on the screen at once. Because most of the team could work most stations, people stepped across to help whenever their own station was under control. By 11:30 the stock was away and I moved to support the dining room.

Once service was on time, I took myself off the stations to manage the whole floor: restocking, temperature checks, breaks two at a time. The day traded about double a normal day.

What it showed

  • Cross-training is what holds on the worst day.
  • Stock temperature comes first, even at peak.
  • The manager's job at peak is the bird's-eye view, as soon as service allows it.

No friction between the team, the managers or the operation, and the team enjoyed the shift as much as I did.

6Delivered

Closing the loop after the doorstep

Guest feedback was read every day and shared, good and bad, in the pre-shift briefing. Low scorers were contacted to put things right. Complaints were handled in person where possible: acknowledge, apologise, act, then a little extra, and a follow-up later.

To see patterns across sites, I built a complaints and delivery-refund tracker with AI tools in September 2025, with a complaints tab and a delivery tab for each of six sites.

Working habits

Six things I do on every shift

Forecast at opening

The day's numbers are checked before the doors open, while there is still time to act on them.

Walk the path

The full travel path every 90 minutes to two hours, peak or not.

Cross-train early

Most people should know most stations before the busy day arrives.

Brief with a purpose

Every pre-shift briefing says what today is for and why each task matters.

Read every review

Guest comments are read daily and shared with the team, good and bad.

Fix the cause

A complaint is logged and traced to its cause, then the guest hears back.
